## North America Nebula
Now we'll look at a massive nebula called the North America Nebula (NGC 7000, Caldwell 20), an emission nebula in the constellation Cygnus named for its North America-like shape. It lies about 2,590 light-years away and is often hard to see with the naked eye, but binoculars and telescopes can show it as a faint, cloudy patch near Deneb.

## Pyramid Cluster

The following image is a picture of the  Pyramid Cluster which is also catalogued as Messier 39 (M39) or  NGC 7092. The bright blue stars are more massive when compared to the other stars. Because massive stars use up their fuel faster, they evolve more quickly; the blue stars still shining in M39 are simply the massive ones that haven’t yet left the main sequence. The white stars are generally cooler and less massive, so they can remain in that stage longer.

## Veil Nebula
The Veil Nebula isn’t a single nebula — it’s a large supernova remnant whose visible filaments have been broken up into multiple cataloged nebulae.

 Astronomers typically divide the Veil Nebula into three major components, plus several smaller knots:

- Western Veil — NGC 6960 (Witch’s Broom)

- Eastern Veil — NGC 6992, NGC 6995, IC 1340

- Pickering’s Triangle — also called Pickering’s Wisp

## Pacman Nebula

NGC 281 is a bright emission nebula and is also known as the Pacman Nebula. Edward Barnard discovered the nebula in 1883. It gets its iconic moniker from a distinct dark lane of dust that cuts across the glowing red hydrogen gas, forming a shape that resembles the classic video game character. This celestial structure is an active star-forming region where new stellar bodies are born. At its core, the nebula houses the young open cluster IC 1590, which provides the energetic radiation that illuminates and shapes the surrounding gas.

## The Bubble Nebula (NGC 7635)

The Bubble Nebula was discovered in November 1787 by the legendary astronomer Sir William Herschel using his large home-built reflector telescope. The fun twist behind this cosmic soap bubble is its extreme violence: the "bubble" is actually being inflated by a runaway central star (BD+60°2522) that is roughly 44 times more massive than our Sun and blistering hot. This monster star unleashes a ferocious stellar wind racing at nearly 4 million miles per hour, plowing into a dense molecular cloud. Because the star is slightly off-center, the expanding gas balloons out unevenly, creating a nearly flawless 7-light-year-wide hollow sphere that looks shockingly fragile against the harsh backdrop of space.

## The Pelican Nebula (IC 5070)

In Cygnus, the Pelican Nebula’s history is tied to astrophotography. William Herschel visually spotted its neighbor, the North America Nebula, in 1786, but the Pelican was too faint and diffuse to map by eye. Astronomers cataloged it in 1908, when John L.E. Dreyer included it in his Second Index Catalog (IC), based on early photographic plates.

The Pelican’s iconic shape is temporary. Hidden newborn stars emit intense ultraviolet radiation, driving an ionization front that erodes the cold gas and dust around them. This process creates the nebula’s long-necked, long-billed appearance, but over millions of years the gas will disperse or be consumed, and the celestial bird will change shape.
